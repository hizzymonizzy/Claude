# Ethics and safety of punishment, decay and loss mechanics in fitness gamification; humane adaptive goals

Scope: adult, single-player, non-competitive, no-money walking game in which the world "cools" a little when weekly
activity is below goal and recovers when above it (Wander World, ADR 0008/0009: ¼ step per bad week, a step shows lost
only after 4 such weeks, floor two steps below best). Research cut-off: November 2026. Evidence was gathered from
search-result abstracts and summaries; full texts were mostly not opened, which is noted where it matters.
One host (from.ncl.ac.uk, a Newcastle University piece on the "dark side" of fitness tech) was blocked by the session
network and could not be read.

## 1. Evidence on compulsive exercise / exercise dependence linked to tracking and gamification

### Takeaway
Tracking reliably increases activity in the short run, but there is solid experimental evidence that it can lower
enjoyment and intrinsic motivation (Etkin 2016) and field evidence that it creates "dependence" (motivation drops when
the tracker is absent), strongest in extrinsically motivated, closure-seeking, low-hope users (Attig & Franke 2019).
Links to disordered eating and compulsive exercise are real but correlational, concentrated in people who track for
weight/shape reasons, and the causal direction is unclear.

### Cited Findings
- Etkin, "The Hidden Cost of Personal Quantification", *Journal of Consumer Research* 42(6):967–984, 2016
  (doi 10.1093/jcr/ucv095): six experiments (colouring, reading, walking) show measurement increases how much people do
  but reduces enjoyment of the activity; mechanism is that measurement shifts attention to output, making enjoyable
  activity feel like work — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1134328);
  [working paper PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Etkin-Jordan-11-12-15-Hidden-Cost.pdf);
  [Duke Fuqua Insights](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps)
- In Etkin's reading study, people who could see their progress read more pages in the session but were less likely to
  keep reading afterwards; when the activity was framed as learning rather than fun, tracking no longer reduced
  enjoyment (framing moderates the harm) — [Duke Fuqua Insights](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps)
- Attig & Franke, "I track, therefore I walk – Exploring the motivational costs of wearing activity trackers in actual
  users", *Int. J. Human-Computer Studies* 127:211–224 (2019): users report a drop in motivation for physical activity
  when the tracker is unavailable ("dependency"); the effect is stronger for people with high extrinsic motivation for
  activity and tracking, high need for cognitive closure, and low hope of success —
  [Univ. Lübeck record](https://www.imis.uni-luebeck.de/de/node/10103);
  [research portal](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/i-track-therefore-i-walk-exploring-the-motivational-costs-of-wear/)
- Attig & Franke, "Abandonment of personal quantification: A review and empirical study investigating reasons for
  wearable activity tracking attrition", *Computers in Human Behavior* 102:223–237 (2019 online / 2020 print), 159
  former users: permanent abandonment is mainly tied to loss of tracking motivation; the paper derives six design
  guidelines for continued use — [Univ. Lübeck record](https://www.imis.uni-luebeck.de/en/node/10100);
  [research portal](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/abandonment-of-personal-quantification-a-review-and-empirical-stu/)
- Simpson & Mazzeo (493 undergraduates): fitness-tracker use predicted higher eating-disorder symptomatology; the
  authors stress self-tracking is "not inherently harmful" — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/fitness-trackers-and-eating-disorders-is-there-a-link-121663);
  [TechRadar summary](https://www.techradar.com/news/do-fitness-trackers-have-an-impact-on-eating-disorders)
- Young-adult survey reported in The Conversation (2019): device users reported higher disordered eating and
  compulsive exercise than non-users; highest levels among those tracking mainly for weight/shape reasons and using
  devices frequently; authors caution people with these tendencies may self-select into tracking —
  [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/fitness-trackers-and-eating-disorders-is-there-a-link-121663)
- 12-week trial of fitness-tracker use during eating-disorder treatment (*Eating and Weight Disorders*, 2022): only a
  small share of participants felt the tracker influenced ED behaviours; perceptions were mixed; results preliminary —
  [PMC9403232](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9403232)
- Wallace, Koebbel & Heath (2025) systematic search and review of food- and activity-tracking technologies and eating
  behaviour includes compulsive exercise among examined outcomes —
  [Univ. Hertfordshire repository](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/27021/2/wallace-et-al-2025-a-systematic-search-and-review-focusing-on-the-influence-of-health-tracking-technologies-on-eating.pdf)
- Exercise addiction is treated in the literature as a behavioural addiction; a narrative overview warns that
  prevalence estimates are inconsistent and inflated by reliance on self-report —
  [Exercise addiction: narrative overview (PMC9869993)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9869993/);
  [Curr. Addiction Reports 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40429-021-00358-8)
- 2026 JMIR qualitative (constructivist grounded theory) study of adults with OCD: streaks, progress bars and tracking
  metrics reinforced "not-just-right" and symmetry compulsions; notifications provoked urges to clear/check —
  [JMIR 2026;e85033](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e85033/PDF)
- Journalism (anecdotal, not evidence): physical therapists report clients "obsessed with closing the rings"; users
  describe guilt about taking a device off —
  [Fortune 2025](https://fortune.com/well/2025/02/04/toxic-relationship-fitness-tracker);
  [TechRadar](https://techradar.com/health-fitness/if-i-feel-guilty-taking-a-device-off-thats-a-warning-sign-how-fitness-trackers-made-me-and-others-like-me-obsessed-with-over-optimization);
  [Refinery29](https://www.refinery29.com/en-gb/fitness-trackers-health-data-anxiety)

### Inferences
- The risk groups map onto traits a game can amplify: Attig & Franke's dependency is strongest with extrinsic
  motivation and need for closure; a "close the week" goal with a visible penalty is exactly an extrinsic, closure-type
  cue. Framing activity as exploration/discovery (Wander World's stated appeal) rather than as quota output is the
  Etkin-consistent mitigation.
- Cooling is a loss signal attached to a number; for a user with ED or OCD tendencies the danger is less the size of
  the loss than its visibility and the "fix it by walking more" incentive. Small, slow, capped losses reduce but don't
  remove that.
- Because walking-only, non-calorie, non-weight framing is used, Wander World avoids the strongest ED correlate
  (tracking for weight/shape). Keeping calories and body metrics out of the game is a meaningful safety choice.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that isolates a weekly *decay* mechanic (vs. tracking alone) as a cause of compulsive
  exercise.
- Original Simpson & Mazzeo paper and the young-adult survey authors were seen only via secondary coverage.
- The Newcastle "dark side of fitness technology" systematic review could not be read (host blocked).

## 2. Dark patterns and ethical guidelines (persuasive tech, gamification, regulators)

### Takeaway
The ethics literature converges on consent, transparency and user benefit: don't persuade users toward what they
wouldn't consent to, avoid exploitation/manipulation, and prefer eudaimonic over coercive design. UK regulators
(CMA/ICO) name confirmshaming, biased framing and harmful nudges/sludge as harmful choice architecture; these are aimed
at commerce and data but are a usable checklist for copy and UI in a health game.

### Cited Findings
- Berdichevsky & Neuenschwander, "Toward an Ethics of Persuasive Technology", *Communications of the ACM*, May 1999,
  pp. 51–58: analyses persuasion as motivations, methods and outcomes; "golden rule": designers should never seek to
  persuade users of something they themselves would not consent to be persuaded to do; the principles act as risk
  indicators (more violated, more ethically risky); responsibility lies with people, not the software —
  [ACM full text](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/1999/5/7904-toward-an-ethics-of-persuasive-technology/fulltext);
  [PDF copy](https://student.cs.uwaterloo.ca/~cs492/08public_html/papers/persuasive.pdf)
- Kim & Werbach, "More than just a game: ethical issues in gamification", *Ethics and Information Technology* 18(2),
  2016: framework of exploitation, manipulation, harms (incl. psychological) and effects on character —
  [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10676-016-9401-5);
  [PDF](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/compsci290.2/spring24/readings/Ethical_issues_in_gamification.pdf)
- Deterding (2014), "Eudaimonic design, or: Six invitations to rethink gamification" (in *Rethinking Gamification*):
  argues gamification should aim beyond "avoiding coercion or harm" toward life-affirming flourishing —
  as quoted by [Butler & Spoelstra 2024](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/168843902/butlerspoelstra2024redemtptionthroughplay.pdf)
- A 2025 *Ethics and Information Technology* article argues gamified systems can manipulate attention and motivation
  without coercion or deceit — [Springer s10676-025-09885-8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-025-09885-8)
- ICO & CMA joint position paper "Harmful design in digital markets" (9 Aug 2023, via DRCF): names harmful nudges and
  sludge, confirmshaming, biased framing, bundled consent and default settings; urges designing around user interests,
  giving choice and control, and evidence-based testing; follows a 2022 CMA paper identifying 21 harmful online choice
  architecture practices — [DRCF PDF](https://www.drcf.org.uk/siteassets/drcf/pdf-files/harmful-design-in-digital-markets-ico-cma-joint-position-paper.pdf);
  [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/ico-and-cma-clamp-down-dark-patterns-uk);
  [Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/dark-patterns/uk-regulators-target-dark-patterns/)
- Alsebayel, Troiano & Harteveld, "Not Nice!" (CHI workshop): deceptive patterns in health apps intersect with users'
  health vulnerability in distinctive ways, so need their own scrutiny — [CEUR Vol-3720](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3720/paper2.pdf)
- Aagaard et al., "A Game of Dark Patterns: Designing Healthy, Highly-Engaging Mobile Games" (CHI 2022 EA), and
  Niknejad et al. "Level Up or Game Over" (MUM '24; 1,496 games): dark patterns are widespread even in games seen as
  benign — [deceptive.design summary](https://deceptive.design/articles/a-game-of-dark-patterns-designing-healthy-highly-engaging-mobile-games);
  [arXiv 2412.05039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.05039)

### Inferences
- Applying the golden rule: the player chose the goal and the cooling rule (it is in the design docs and agreed), so
  consent is present; it stays ethical if the rule is visible, explained, and adjustable/pausable by the player.
- Confirmshaming and biased framing are the realistic risks in copy: e.g. "Your world is dying" or a pause button
  labelled "I give up". Neutral, factual wording ("The world cooled slightly last week") avoids both.
- Engagement-maximising patterns that are dark in games (fear of missing out, daily login pressure, notifications that
  provoke checking) are worth avoiding even with no money involved, since the harm path is psychological, not financial.

### Gaps
- WHO and NHS digital-health guidance (e.g. NHS DTAC, NICE evidence standards framework) were not retrieved in this
  pass; I could not confirm whether they say anything specific about punishment/decay mechanics. I'd expect them to
  focus on clinical safety, data and evidence rather than game mechanics, but this is unverified.
- The UK Online Safety Act was not checked; it targets user-to-user and search services and is unlikely to apply to a
  single-player offline app (inference, not sourced).

## 3. Reception of decay / withering mechanics (Forest, Habitica, Tamagotchi-style pets, Finch)

### Takeaway
The only rigorous study found (Habitica) shows punishment mechanics produce counterproductive effects for everyone in
the sample, notably being punished during productive periods when tasks couldn't be checked off, and that perceived
unfairness of the reward/penalty system predicts motivation loss. Forest's dying tree is widely credited (anecdotally)
with working via loss aversion, but it penalises a short, voluntary, in-the-moment choice, not a week of life. Finch
deliberately avoids penalties for missed days.

### Cited Findings
- Diefenbach & Müssig, "Counterproductive effects of gamification: An analysis on the example of the gamified task
  manager Habitica", *IJHCS* 127:190–210 (2019), doi 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2018.09.004: Study 1 (single-user IPA interview)
  found seven themes of counterproductive effects plus seven themes on the reward/punishment system and psychological
  reactions; Study 2 (45 users, 2 weeks): all participants experienced counterproductive effects to some degree; a
  common one was being punished during especially productive times because tasks couldn't be checked off in time;
  prevalence correlated with perceived inappropriateness of the reward system, which predicted motivation change over
  time — [LMU ePub](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/77668); [peeref](https://peeref.com/works/10698835)
- A JMIR Serious Games 2024 paper cites a study finding Habitica's point system "actively harmful" —
  [JMIR Serious Games 2024 e43078](https://games.jmir.org/2024/1/e43078/PDF)
- Competitor commentary (commercial, low weight) claims Habitica HP loss "creates anxiety, not motivation" —
  [habi.app](https://habi.app/insights/habitica-alternatives/)
- Forest: leaving the app mid-session kills the tree, which stays in your forest as a dead tree; users can tap "give
  up"; credited to loss aversion by reviewers; no controlled trials found —
  [Forest docs](https://forestapp.cc/llms-full.txt); [Headway review](https://makeheadway.com/blog/forest-app-review/);
  [Octalysis case study](https://medium.com/@himanshukhemani/octalysis-gamification-of-forest-app-ux-case-study-56e3e382714b)
- Finch: streak counts every day you open the app ("just checking in is enough") —
  [Finch help center](https://help.finchcare.com/hc/en-us/articles/37780736136205-Understanding-Streaks);
  reviewers note missed days aren't penalised and the bird doesn't suffer —
  [Habitbox review](https://habitbox.app/blog/finch-app-review); a design critique notes actions can be scaled,
  replaced, postponed or removed without penalty — [Pratt IxD critique 2026](https://ixd.prattsi.org/2026/02/design-critique-finch-self-care-pet-ios-app/);
  a critical essay argues even Finch's emphasis on daily consistency can create guilt for some —
  [Vocal critique](https://vocal.media/critique/the-criousity-of-the-finch-app)
- Users of Finch praise the absence of leaderboards and self-set goals —
  [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/apps/this-self-care-virtual-pet-is-helping-me-get-my-act-together-160027169.html);
  [Internet Matters](https://www.internetmatters.org/advice/apps-and-platforms/wellbeing/finch/)

### Inferences
- The Habitica finding about being punished for things outside one's control (couldn't check off in time) maps
  directly onto illness, injury, travel, watch not worn, or Health Connect sync gaps in a walking game. Cooling must not
  trigger on data gaps the player didn't cause, and should be pausable for life events.
- Perceived fairness is the key moderator: small, predictable, explained, recoverable losses are more likely to read as
  fair than sudden or opaque ones. Wander World's ¼-step, 4-week lag, floor and symmetric recovery are in line with this.
- Forest's model works because the loss is tied to an immediate, voluntary choice; weekly cooling is tied to a
  week of life with many involuntary causes, so it should be softer than Forest's death, which it already is.
- Nothing irreversible: dead Forest trees remain forever; Wander World's floor and recovery avoid permanent marks, which
  is the safer choice.

### Gaps
- No Finch founder interview or post-mortem was found stating their reasoning; only third-party readings.
- No GDC talk or designer post-mortem on decay mechanics in fitness games was located in this pass (e.g. Pokémon GO
  buddy, Pikmin Bloom, Zombies, Run! — none checked).
- Duolingo streak-freeze/churn data was searched for but not found.

## 4. Patterns for rest, illness and injury (pause, sick days, windows, floors, recovery, fresh starts)

### Takeaway
The clearest industry signal is Apple's 2024 watchOS 11 change: after years of user complaints, Activity rings gained
a pause (day/week/month, streaks preserved) and per-weekday goals, explicitly for rest, illness and injury. Research
supports adaptive/personal goals over fixed norms for chronic illness, and the "fresh start effect" supports using
temporal landmarks (new week) for re-engagement.

### Cited Findings
- watchOS 11 (2024) lets users pause Activity rings for a day, week, month or more "without affecting their award
  streaks"; covered as sick/recovery days for rings —
  [iMore](https://www.imore.com/health-fitness/apple-watch/how-to-pause-activity-streaks-in-watchos-11);
  [heise](https://heise.de/-9955575); [BGR](https://bgr.com/tech/with-watchos-11-pausing-my-activity-rings-wont-make-me-feel-bad-about-myself-anymore/)
- Pause duration: some sources report a 90-day maximum; Apple's wording suggests open-ended — conflicting, unresolved —
  [Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/wellness/smartwatches/how-to-pause-activity-rings-on-your-apple-watch);
  [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2024/12/31/watchos-11-fixes-easier-apple-watch-health-goals/)
- watchOS 11 also allows different goals per weekday and a one-day adjustment that snaps back next day —
  [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2024/06/18/watchos-11-custom-activity-goals-different-days);
  [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/health-fitness/fitness-trackers/latest-watchos-11-beta-feature-found-and-its-perfect-for-scheduling-in-lazy-days)
- Dai, Milkman & Riis, "The Fresh Start Effect", *Management Science* 60(10):2563–2582 (2014): diet searches, gym visits
  and goal commitments rise after temporal landmarks (new week, month, year, birthdays, holidays); landmarks create a
  psychological break from past imperfections; the effect may be short-lived —
  [PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf);
  [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/need-fresh-start-heres-begin/)
- MS pilot (JMIR mHealth 2018) used symptom-based "sweet spot" step goals rather than fixed targets; ~one-third dropped
  out and only half met targets on ≥50% of days — personalised goals are not a full fix —
  [JMIR mHealth 2018;6(4):e70](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/4/e70/PDF)
- In 130 people with chronic disease, step counts from nine commercial trackers correlated poorly with the gold
  standard during daily activities (impaired gait causes miscounts) —
  [PMC5902695](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5902695/)
- Adults over 50 with chronic illness found wearables useful and acceptable but needed support to interpret data —
  [JMIR mHealth 2016](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2016/1/e7/PDF)

### Inferences
- A safe default set for Wander World, consistent with the above: (a) a one-tap rest/sick/injury pause that freezes
  cooling (not recovery) with no justification required and no shame copy; (b) don't count weeks with missing sensor
  data as below goal; (c) keep the floor and the "loss shows only after 4 weeks" lag; (d) let recovery be at least as
  fast as decay (currently symmetric ¼ step; faster recovery would further reduce lock-in); (e) use the Tuesday
  scoring as a fresh-start moment with positive copy; (f) let the player lower or reshape the goal at any time.
- Weekly rather than daily windows already absorb single rest days, which is the main argument for weekly goals.
  A rolling window smooths more but makes the "week turned over" fresh-start landmark weaker; trade-off, no direct
  evidence found either way.
- Because step/activity counting is less accurate for impaired gait, a game that penalises shortfall penalises
  measurement error for disabled users; using active minutes (as Wander World does) rather than steps helps, and an
  opt-out of cooling entirely is the accessible fallback.

### Gaps
- No study compared pause features, floors or recovery-faster-than-decay designs on wellbeing or adherence directly;
  the recommendations above are inferences from adjacent evidence and industry practice.
- No official disability-specific guidance on fitness-game goals was found.
- Attig & Franke's six design guidelines (abandonment paper) were not read in full.

## 5. Self-compassion and framing after missed goals

### Takeaway
Consistent (mostly correlational) evidence that self-compassionate responses to lapses go with less negative affect
and faster re-engagement with exercise goals; perfectionists are least likely to respond self-compassionately, which
is the group a punitive mechanic would hurt most.

### Cited Findings
- Semenchuk, Strachan & Fortier, *Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology* 40:31–39 (2018), 105 adults,
  cross-sectional recalled setbacks: self-compassion predicted unique variance in exercise-goal re-engagement and lower
  negative affect, beyond self-esteem — [PDF](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Semenchuk2018.pdf)
- Drexel study in *Appetite* (2024), smartphone ecological momentary assessment of eating/exercise lapses: more
  self-compassionate responses were followed by better mood and self-control in following hours; team says it helped
  people resume goals faster — [Drexel news](https://drexel.edu/news/archive/2024/january/can-practicing-self-compassion-help-people-achieve-weight-loss-goals);
  [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1032409)
- Study of 220 exercisers: self-compassion linked to fewer negative emotional responses to recalled lapses and more
  intrinsic motivation, moderated by gender-role schemas —
  [record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi.dedup.....a8fbc0746546a0275c32f82df91c55b7)
- Biskas et al., *British Journal of Social Psychology* (2022), pre-registered prospective study: people with negative
  beliefs about self-compassion were less likely to respond to lapses compassionately; those high in perfectionistic
  concerns held more such beliefs —
  [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/184697/1/British%20J%20Social%20Psychol%20-%202022%20-%20Biskas%20-%20Using%20social%20cognition%20models%20to%20understand%20why%20people%20%20such%20as%20perfectionists%20.pdf)
- Etkin's framing moderator (learning vs fun) and Fresh Start landmarks (above) are further evidence that framing
  changes how measurement and lapses are experienced —
  [Duke Fuqua](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps);
  [Dai et al.](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)

### Inferences
- Copy for a below-goal week should normalise ("weeks vary"), be specific and small ("a little cooler"), point forward
  ("one good week brings it back"), and never moralise. Avoid red/alarm styling and loss-framed notifications.
- Showing what was *kept* (best level, floor, places discovered that never fade) alongside the cooling supports a
  self-compassionate reading and fits Finch's "pause, not failure" stance.
- Perfectionists are both most attracted to closure mechanics (Attig & Franke) and least self-compassionate (Biskas),
  so an opt-out of cooling and a pause are not niche features; they protect the most at-risk users.

### Gaps
- No experimental study was found that tests self-compassionate in-app copy specifically on resumption after a missed
  weekly fitness goal; evidence is from general exercise/diet lapses and is largely correlational.
