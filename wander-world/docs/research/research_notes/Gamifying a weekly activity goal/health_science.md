# Health-science basis for a personal weekly physical-activity goal (one healthy adult, walking game)

Method note: who.int, health.gov, ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and biomedcentral.com could not be fetched from this session (DNS blocked by the session's network allowlist). The findings below come from search-result abstracts and summaries of the primary papers and guidelines, with the primary URLs cited. Exact guideline wording that I could not check against the primary text is flagged. Searched November 2026.

## 1. What WHO (2020) and US HHS PAG (2nd ed., 2018) recommend

### Takeaway
Both guidelines set the same adult aerobic target: 150–300 min/week of moderate activity, or 75–150 min/week of vigorous activity, or an equivalent mix (1 vigorous minute = 2 moderate minutes). Both say some activity is better than none and that bouts of any length count. Both also say to sit less: WHO says swapping sitting for activity of any intensity, light included, has benefits, but neither guideline sets a light-activity or sitting threshold. A weekly MVPA-minute target is therefore the best-supported unit. A sensible 'full goal' is 150 moderate-equivalent minutes, with a stretch level at 300.

### Cited Findings
- WHO 2020 wording: "Adults should do at least 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity; or at least 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic physical activity; or an equivalent combination of moderate- and vigorous-intensity activity throughout the week." Strong recommendation, moderate-certainty evidence. — [Bull et al., WHO 2020 guidelines, Br J Sports Med 2020;54:1451–1462](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/24/1451); [PMC7719906](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/)
- WHO 2020 repeats the messages that "some physical activity is better than none" and that "more physical activity is better for optimal health outcomes". Adults who fall short of the target still benefit from doing some activity. — [Bull et al. 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/24/1451)
- Sedentary behaviour (new in 2020): adults should limit time spent sedentary. Replacing sitting with activity of any intensity, including light intensity, has health benefits. Evidence was "insufficient to quantify a sedentary behaviour threshold". — [Bull et al. 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/24/1451)
- WHO 2020 also recommends muscle-strengthening activity on 2 or more days a week for all age groups. A walking game would not cover this. — [Bull et al. 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/24/1451)
- PAG 2018 sets the same adult aerobic target (150–300 min moderate or 75–150 min vigorous), plus muscle strengthening on 2 or more days a week. — [Piercy et al., JAMA 2018 (doi 10.1001/jama.2018.14854)](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2712935)
- PAG 2018 dropped the 2008 rule that activity had to come in bouts of at least 10 minutes. The new wording is that "bouts, or episodes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity of any duration may be included in the daily accumulated total volume". — [APTA summary of PAG 2018](https://apta.org/news/2018/11/13/new-physical-activity-guidelines-stress-the-importance-of-movement-of-any-duration); [ACSM blog, Haskell 2019](https://acsm.org/blog-detail/acsm-blog/2019/07/09/physical-activity-decreased-risk-mortality-cardiovascular-disease-short-bouts-count)
- PAG 2018 Executive Summary: "Adults should move more and sit less throughout the day. Some physical activity is better than none. Adults who sit less and do any amount of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity gain some health benefits." — [PAG Executive Summary (health.gov PDF)](https://health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-10/PAG_ExecutiveSummary.pdf); [Harvard Health summary](https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/new-physical-activity-guidelines-even-a-little-activity-will-help-health)
- Inactive people should start with lower-intensity activity and increase how often and how long they are active over time. Replacing sitting with light activity, such as a slow walk, is a suggested first step before moving to more intense activity. — [Colorado State summary of PAG 2nd ed.](https://foodsmartcolorado.colostate.edu/nutrition-and-health/physical-activity/physical-activity-basics/overview-of-physical-activity-guidelines-for-americans-2nd-edition); [Harvard Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2018/11/13/new-physical-activity-guidelines-for-americans/)
- ACSM 2011 position stand: at least 30 min/day of moderate activity on at least 5 days/week (150 min/week or more), or at least 20 min/day of vigorous activity on at least 3 days/week. "Gradual progression of exercise time, frequency and intensity is recommended for best adherence and least injury risk." People who cannot meet the minimum still benefit from some activity. — [Garber et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2011;43:1334–59 (PubMed 21694556)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)

### Inferences
- A weekly goal measured in moderate-equivalent minutes (vigorous minutes counted double) matches both guidelines directly. Fitbit's Active Zone Minutes use this same 1:2 weighting (see section 2).
- The guidelines give a floor (150) and a ceiling for further benefit (300). That suggests two tiers: 'goal met' at 150 and a bonus tier up to 300. Since "some is better than none", a partial week should still earn credit rather than count as a failure.
- Because bouts of any length now count, the game should count short brisk segments, not only long walks.

### Gaps
- I could not read the primary health.gov PDF. I therefore could not confirm the exact PAG wording on spreading activity "throughout the week", or whether it gives a numeric progression (for example, add a set number of minutes every few weeks).
- Neither guideline quantifies a light-intensity or sitting target.

## 2. Does light-intensity walking count? Steps versus minutes versus intensity

### Takeaway
Daily step volume has a strong, dose-response link to lower mortality that levels off around 6,000–8,000 steps/day for people aged 60+ and 8,000–10,000 for under-60s. Benefit starts well below 10,000 steps, at roughly 2,300–4,400 steps/day. After adjusting for total steps, step intensity (cadence) added no significant independent benefit in the main cohorts. So slow walking does count, through volume. The guideline unit, however, is MVPA minutes, and cadence of about 100 steps/min or more is the accepted proxy for moderate intensity. All of this evidence is observational.

### Cited Findings
- **Paluch et al. 2022, Lancet Public Health** (meta-analysis of 15 cohorts, 47,471 adults, 3,013 deaths, median follow-up 7.1 years). Highest vs lowest step quartile: adjusted HR for death 0.47 (95% CI 0.39–0.57). Risk fell as steps rose and levelled off at about 6,000–8,000 steps/day in adults 60+ and about 8,000–10,000 in adults under 60. Time spent walking at 40 steps/min or faster was not significant once total steps were accounted for. — [PubMed 35247352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35247352/); [UCL full text](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10170017/1/1-s2.0-S2468266721003029-main.pdf)
- **Lee et al. 2019, JAMA Intern Med** (16,741 older women, Women's Health Study, mean follow-up 4.3 years). Women averaging about 4,400 steps/day had lower mortality than those averaging about 2,700. Mortality fell further as steps rose and levelled off at about 7,500 steps/day. Measures of stepping intensity were no longer significant after adjusting for steps/day. — [PubMed 31141585](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31141585/); [Stanford PDF](https://whish.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Step-Volume-Intensity-Mortality_JAMA-IntMedicine-2019-.pdf)
- **Saint-Maurice et al. 2020, JAMA** (NHANES, a representative sample of US adults aged 40+). More daily steps were associated with lower all-cause mortality. There was no significant association between step intensity and mortality after adjusting for total steps. — [PMC7093766](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7093766)
- **Banach et al. 2023, Eur J Prev Cardiol** (17 cohorts, 226,889 people, median follow-up 7.1 years). Mortality benefit started at about 3,967 steps/day for all causes and about 2,337 steps/day for cardiovascular death. Each extra 1,000 steps/day was linked to about 15% lower all-cause mortality, and each extra 500 steps/day to about 7% lower cardiovascular mortality. The sources disagree slightly: an LJMU repository copy gives 3,867 rather than 3,967 for the all-cause figure. — [ESC press release](https://escardio.org/The-ESC/Press-Office/Press-releases/World-s-largest-study-shows-the-more-you-walk-the-lower-your-risk-of-death-even-if-you-walk-fewer-than-5-000-steps); [LJMU repository](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/22970/)
- **Cadence as an intensity proxy (CADENCE-Adults).** About 100 steps/min is the heuristic threshold for moderate intensity (3 METs) and about 130 steps/min for vigorous (6 METs) in adults aged 21–40. The same thresholds held for ages 41–60. The 100 steps/min threshold had a positive predictive value of 91.4%. For ages 61–85, 100, 110 and 120 steps/min corresponded to 3, 4 and 5 METs. Thresholds for relative intensity (heart rate or perceived exertion) were higher: about 105–120 steps/min for moderate. — [Tudor-Locke et al., IJBNPA 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-019-0769-6); [UMass news](https://www.umass.edu/news/article/tudor-locke-examines-walking-cadence)
- **Fitbit Active Zone Minutes (AZM).** Heart-rate zones are personalised from age and resting heart rate: fat burn (default 50–69% of max), cardio (70–84%) and peak (85% and above). A fat-burn minute counts as 1 AZM; a cardio or peak minute counts as 2. The default goal is 150 AZM per week. Everyday activity such as a brisk walk starts earning AZM after 7 minutes in a zone. — [Google/Fitbit support](https://support.google.com/fitbit/answer/14236509); [Fitbit Luxe manual](https://help.fitbit.com/manuals/luxe/Content/manuals/Topics/Exercise%20and%20Heart%20Health/Check%20your%20heart%20rate.htm); [Fit&Well summary of default zones](https://fitandwell.com/features/what-are-fitbit-active-zone-minutes)
- **Apple Watch.** The Exercise ring needs "at least 30 minutes of activity at or above a brisk walk". What counts as brisk is personalised using cardio fitness. Lighter movement counts only toward the Move (calorie) ring. Apple Watch suggests a new Move goal each week based on recent activity, which is a commercial example of a weekly adaptive goal. — [Apple, Close Your Rings](https://www.apple.com/watch/close-your-rings/); [Apple Community thread quoting support doc (unofficial)](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/256204384)

### Inferences
- Slow walking below 100 steps/min carries real benefit through step volume, especially for people starting from low step counts, but it does not count toward guideline MVPA minutes. A game could count total distance or steps (volume) toward progress and count minutes at or above about 100 steps/min (or GPS speed of roughly 4.5–5 km/h or more) toward the weekly "health goal". Alternatively it could give light walking partial weight. The speed equivalence is my estimate and is not sourced.
- Saint-Maurice and Lee found no independent benefit from intensity, and Paluch found none from walking at 40 steps/min or faster. A goal that strictly requires brisk pace could therefore undervalue slow walkers. A hybrid (volume plus brisk minutes) is defensible.
- Fitbit (1x/2x weighting, 150 per week) and Apple (brisk-walk threshold, weekly Move-goal suggestion) both put the WHO/PAG target into practice. Matching them keeps the game consistent with what users already know.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Apple's official support page on the definition of brisk, or any validation of Fitbit AZM against measured METs.
- I found no RCT showing that light-intensity walking alone improves hard outcomes. The evidence is observational and open to reverse causation, since sicker people walk less.

## 3. Adaptive or personalised goals versus fixed goals

### Takeaway
Small RCTs generally favour adaptive goals, which move up or down with recent performance, over a fixed 10,000-step goal. Effects are modest: about 1,000 steps/day net, rising to about 16 extra MVPA min/day when combined with immediate rewards. The best-documented algorithm sets the goal at the 60th percentile of the last 9 days of measured activity, so the goal rises slowly and falls automatically after low weeks. One 2017 factorial trial reported mixed results. I found no recent meta-analysis that isolates adaptive goals.

### Cited Findings
- **Adams et al. 2013, PLoS ONE** (RCT, 20 overweight adults). Goals were adapted from a moving window of the person's own performance. Steps rose by 2,728/day in the adaptive group and 1,598/day in the static group, a significant between-group difference of 1,130 steps/day. — [PubMed 24349392](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24349392/); [ASU PDF](https://keep.lib.asu.edu/system/files/c160/Adaptive_Physical_Activity_2013.pdf)
- **Adaptive algorithm (WalkIT protocol).** "Rank, from lowest to highest, the measurements in a 9-day moving window and compute a goal based on a percentile criterion (eg, 60th)." — [WalkIT protocol, JMIR Res Protoc 2015](https://researchprotocols.org/2015/3/e108/PDF)
- **Adams et al. 2017, BMC Public Health** (2×2 factorial RCT over 4 months: adaptive vs static goals, immediate vs delayed rewards; 96 inactive overweight adults per my recollection of the paper, unverified). The abstract concludes that adaptive goals outperformed static 10,000-step goals over 4 months. However, from baseline to the first intervention phase, the static group gained more (2,630 vs 2,149 steps/day; difference 482, p = .095, not significant). Small immediate rewards beat larger delayed rewards. — [PMC5372290](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5372290/); [BMC PDF](https://bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12889-017-4197-8)
- **Zhou et al. 2018, JMIR mHealth uHealth (CalFit)** (10-week RCT, 64 adults). Machine-learning (behavioural analytics) personalised daily step goals were compared with a fixed 10,000/day goal. Steps fell in both groups, by 390 (SD 490) in the intervention group and 1,350 (SD 420) in the control group (P = .03). The net difference was 960 steps/day (95% CI 90–1,830). There was no natural baseline because intervention began during run-in. The authors called it short-term efficacy that needs a larger, longer RCT. — [JMIR mHealth 2018;6(1):e28](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/1/e28/); [PMC5806006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5806006)
- **WalkIT Arizona** (2×2 factorial RCT, 512 insufficiently active adults, 12 months: adaptive vs static goals × immediate vs delayed reinforcement; NCT02717663). The adaptive-goal plus immediate-reinforcement group gained about 16.5 min/day of accelerometer MVPA, more than either delayed-reinforcement group. A 2023 IJBNPA analysis found that adaptive goals increased the likelihood of any MVPA and daily MVPA minutes, especially in less walkable neighbourhoods. — [PMC8820277](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8820277); [IJBNPA 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s12966-023-01494-2); [JMIR Form Res 2020 (domain-specific walking)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7748953/)
- **Meta-regression of 50 mHealth trials (N = 5,997).** Including behavioural goals or self-monitoring was associated with greater intervention success. The analysis did not separate adaptive from static goals. — [PMC6256104](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6256104/)
- A 2020s trial on the mechanisms of action of adaptive goal setting is registered at NCT04505241. I found no published results. — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04505241](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04505241)

### Inferences
- A 60th-percentile rule over a recent window is the evidence-backed default. It sets a goal that is a little above typical recent performance. It rises gradually when the person improves and falls automatically after illness or a lull, without any special illness logic. For a weekly game the analogue would be a percentile, or a slightly-above-median value, of the last few weeks' weekly totals. Transferring the rule from days to weeks is my extrapolation and was not tested in these trials.
- Pairing adaptive goals with small, immediate rewards (in-game feedback) showed the strongest effects. Delayed or large rewards did worse.
- Capping the adaptive goal at the guideline target, for example 150–300 moderate-equivalent min/week, is reasonable. The guidelines show little evidence of extra benefit for a general adult above 300 min, so an endlessly rising goal is not needed.

### Gaps
- I could not access the full text of Adams 2017, so I could not confirm the exact N, the follow-up results, or whether the algorithm used a floor or cap.
- I found no RCT that directly tests how fast a goal should rise (step sizes) or a specific rule for lowering it after illness. The percentile method handles both only implicitly.
- I found no "Chen et al. 2023 just-in-time adaptive goal" trial. Reviews of just-in-time adaptive interventions note that few randomized studies exist. — [PMC7850911 (JITAI review, per search summary)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7850911)

## 4. Safe progression, rest, illness and overtraining for walkers

### Takeaway
The guidelines say "start low, go slow": raise duration and frequency before intensity, in small steps. The popular "10% per week" rule lacks evidence. A trial in novice runners found no injury difference between about 10% progression and faster progression. A cohort study suggested that large spikes (more than 30% over two weeks) carry more risk. For walking at guideline volumes the injury and overtraining risk is low. The main risks are sudden spikes and exercising with a fever or systemic illness. The traditional "neck check" heuristic for illness is widely used but has weak evidence.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM: "Gradual progression of exercise time, frequency and intensity is recommended for best adherence and least injury risk." — [Garber et al. 2011, PubMed 21694556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)
- PAG 2nd ed. (secondary summaries): inactive people should begin with lower-intensity activities and gradually increase how often and how long they are active. — [Colorado State](https://foodsmartcolorado.colostate.edu/nutrition-and-health/physical-activity/physical-activity-basics/overview-of-physical-activity-guidelines-for-americans-2nd-edition); [CSUMB](https://csumb.edu/kinesiology/exercise-is-medicine-on-campus/physical-activity-guidelines/)
- **Buist et al. 2008** (RCT, 532 novice runners, graded programme of about 10% per week vs standard programme). Injury rates were 20.8% (52/250) vs 20.3% (48/236), so there was no benefit from the 10% programme. Summaries differ on how fast the comparison group progressed (sources give 50% or 23.7%). — [Running-physio summary](https://www.running-physio.com/skimpyresearch/); [Outside, "Myth of the 10 percent rule"](https://www.outsideonline.com/health/running/training-advice/running-101/myth-of-the-10-percent-rule/?scope=anon)
- **Nielsen et al. 2014** (cohort, 874 novice runners with GPS). Cox regression found no statistically significant difference in injury rates across the three progression groups. Runners who increased distance by more than 30% over two weeks appeared more vulnerable than those who increased by less than 10%. — [Aalborg University record](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/excessive-progression-in-weekly-running-distance-and-risk-of-runn)
- **Systematic review.** The "10% rule" is not justified. Evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or changes in training to running injury is conflicting. Later Aarhus work suggests that single-session spikes (the longest single run) matter more than week-to-week change. — [PMC9528699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9528699); [BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751)
- **Illness and the "neck check".** The traditional advice: with symptoms only above the neck (runny nose, sore throat) light training may be acceptable if the person feels well. With symptoms below the neck (fever, chest symptoms, vomiting) the person should not exercise. Sports-medicine sources note there is "no scientific evidence" for the neck check and that its validity has been challenged. The common clinical approach is a staged return (walking first) that gradually increases load and manages post-viral fatigue. Training during a viral infection is linked to a risk of myocarditis (popular source). — [University of Pretoria SEMLI](https://www.up.ac.za/Sport-Exercise-Medicine-and-Lifestyle-Institute/news/post_3042925-medical-guidance-for-athletes-returning-to-sport-after-an-acute-respiratory-illness); [USA Swimming / sports physician](https://www.usaswimming.org/news/2022/01/10/when-can-young-athletes-return-to-sport-after-illness); [PMC9711907 staged return table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9711907/table/t0010); [CASEM 2024 consensus on acute respiratory illness](https://www.casem-acmse.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/1066.full_.pdf)

### Inferences
- Goal-rise logic should avoid sudden large jumps. A weekly increase in the 5–15% range, with no single-week jump of about 30% or more, fits the spirit of the evidence. The exact numbers are a design choice: no study validates a specific percentage for walkers.
- After a low week (illness, travel) the goal should drop back toward recent actual activity, as the percentile method does, rather than demanding the old level. The game should never push the user to "make up" missed minutes in one spike, which is the pattern linked to running injuries.
- Rest days are compatible with weekly goals (see section 5). The game should not penalise a day off. A "sick or rest week" pause or freeze that does not break streaks would be consistent with the guidance not to exercise with fever or systemic symptoms. The game should avoid giving medical advice beyond a generic "if you have a fever or feel unwell, rest".
- The running-injury evidence comes from runners, whose loading is higher than walking. Applying it to walkers is conservative.

### Gaps
- I found no walker-specific data on overtraining or on injury from progression rates. The overtraining literature concerns athletes.
- I could not read the full CASEM 2024 consensus recommendations.

## 5. Weekly versus daily goals: weekend-warrior evidence

### Takeaway
For the same total weekly MVPA, packing it into 1–2 days ("weekend warrior") is associated with mortality benefits similar to spreading it across 3 or more days. That supports a flexible weekly total over a rigid daily quota. Both supporting studies are observational.

### Cited Findings
- **O'Donovan et al. 2017, JAMA Intern Med 177(3):335–342** (pooled 11 cohorts, about 63,600 adults from the England and Scotland health surveys per the paper, sample size not re-verified). Weekend warriors (1–2 sessions/week meeting the guideline) and regularly active people (3 or more sessions) both had lower mortality than inactive people. CVD mortality HR was 0.60 (0.45–0.82) for weekend warriors and 0.59 (0.48–0.73) for the regularly active. "Insufficiently active" people (some activity below the guideline) also had lower risk than inactive people. That last point is from my recollection of the paper and was not verified in the search summary. — [UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1539729/1/O'Donovan_Association_Weekend_Warrior.pdf); [Healio summary](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20170109/leisure-time-physical-activity-patterns-yield-mortality-benefits)
- **dos Santos et al. 2022, JAMA Intern Med** (US National Health Interview Survey cohort). All-cause mortality HR vs inactive was 0.92 (0.83–1.02, not significant) for weekend warriors and 0.85 (0.83–0.88) for the regularly active. "Given the same amount of total MVPA, weekend warrior participants had similar all-cause and cause-specific mortality rates as regularly active participants." — [PMC9257680](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9257680); [PubMed 35788615](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35788615/); [Healio summary](https://cm.healio.com/news/primary-care/20220711/physical-activity-confers-similar-benefits-when-spread-out-or-concentrated-in-fewer-days)
- WHO 2020 frames the target per week ("throughout the week"), and PAG 2018 counts bouts of any duration toward accumulated volume. — [Bull et al. 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/24/1451); [APTA](https://apta.org/news/2018/11/13/new-physical-activity-guidelines-stress-the-importance-of-movement-of-any-duration)
- The adaptive-goal RCTs in section 3 all used daily step goals, and Apple's Exercise ring is daily. Fitbit AZM (150/week) and Apple's Move-goal suggestion are weekly. — [Google/Fitbit](https://support.google.com/fitbit/answer/14236509); [Apple](https://www.apple.com/watch/close-your-rings/)

### Inferences
- A weekly total with free distribution across days is health-equivalent to daily quotas and more forgiving of work schedules and weather. It is a sound basis for the game. Daily nudges can still be used as motivational sub-goals.
- The progression caution in section 4 still applies. Concentrating a big week into one very long walk is a single-session spike. A soft per-session cap, or diminishing credit per session, may be prudent. This is design inference, not evidence-based.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT comparing weekly with daily goal framing for adherence or for step and minute outcomes in app interventions.
- In dos Santos 2022 the weekend-warrior all-cause HR was not statistically significant. Its confidence interval is wide.
