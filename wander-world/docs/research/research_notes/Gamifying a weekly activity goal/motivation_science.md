# Motivation and behaviour-change science for gamifying a personal physical-activity goal

Scope: adults, walking / general physical activity (PA), single-player non-competitive game that rewards activity with world growth. Researched 2026-11-23. Note: pubmed.gov and mhealth.jmir.org could not be fetched from this session, so some figures come from search-result abstracts/secondary coverage rather than full text (flagged where relevant).

## Self-determination theory and the overjustification / crowding-out effect

### Takeaway
More autonomous (self-endorsed, enjoyment- or value-based) motivation consistently predicts exercise, and games are compelling largely because they satisfy autonomy, competence and relatedness. Tangible rewards that are expected and contingent on performance undermine intrinsic motivation in lab studies, while informational positive feedback raises it; in PA specifically, however, modest cash incentives have not been shown to leave people worse off than controls after removal.

### Cited Findings
- Teixeira et al. 2012 systematic review (66 studies to June 2011): "consistent support for a positive relation between more autonomous forms of motivation and exercise"; identified regulation (valuing the outcome) predicted initial/short-term adoption more strongly than intrinsic motivation; evidence weaker for health/fitness/body motives and introjected (guilt-driven) regulation. Mostly non-experimental designs. — [Teixeira et al. 2012, IJBNPA (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3441783/); [PubMed 22726453](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22726453/)
- Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006 (four studies): the motivational "pull" of video games is largely due to their capacity to engender feelings of autonomy, competence and relatedness; not all games satisfy these needs equally. — [Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006, Motivation and Emotion (PDF)](https://www.rochester.edu/warner/lida/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/02bfe513dd59366750000000.pdf); [Rochester press release via ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/12/061226134706.htm)
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 meta-analysis (128 experiments): performance-contingent rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28 across reported analyses); positive feedback enhanced it (d = 0.33 free-choice behaviour); tangible rewards more detrimental for children than college students. — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, Psychol Bull (PDF)](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); [PubMed 10589297](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/)
- The size of the undermining effect is contested: Cameron & Pierce (1994) concluded negative effects of reward were limited and easily prevented in applied settings; Deci et al. argue effects are pervasive. — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001, Review of Educational Research](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- Exercise-specific crowding-out check: in Mitchell et al.'s meta-analysis of 23 RCTs (6,074 adults), incentives were associated with *higher* mean daily steps after incentives were withdrawn (pooled MD +513.8 steps, 95% CI 312.7–714.9), although only 4 of 18 studies individually reported post-intervention benefit. — [Mitchell et al. 2020, BJSM (PDF)](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/54/21/1259.full.pdf)
- In Patel 2016 (loss vs gain cash incentives), steps decreased in all incentive groups after the incentives ended but were "not different from control" — i.e. a return to baseline, not a drop below it. — [Healio summary of Patel et al. 2016](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20160217/financial-loss-incentive-most-effective-at-increasing-physical-activity)

### Inferences
- For world growth as the "reward": it is a symbolic, non-monetary, informational reward (it shows progress/competence), closer to Deci et al.'s "positive feedback" category (which helped) than to tangible performance-contingent payments (which hurt). Framing growth as a reflection of the player's exploration rather than as payment for steps is the SDT-consistent choice.
- Autonomy suggests letting the player choose goals, routes and what grows; competence suggests visible, attainable progression; relatedness is hard in a single-player game but could come from narrative/world characters.
- The "after the reward ends" risk for a game is less about undermining and more about simple decay to baseline when the game stops being opened.

### Gaps
- Could not find a field RCT directly testing whether non-monetary in-game rewards undermine intrinsic motivation for walking in adults.
- Did not retrieve the exact tangible-reward summary effect (often quoted as about d = -0.34) from the 1999 tables; not verified.

## Loss aversion, loss- vs gain-framed incentives, and decay mechanics

### Takeaway
Loss-framed incentives (money given upfront and taken away when a goal is missed) beat equivalent gain-framed ones for meeting daily step goals during the intervention, but effects largely vanish once incentives stop; behaviourally designed gamification (points/levels with loss framing plus social elements) increased steps by about 600–900/day, with only the competitive arm sustaining a benefit at 12 weeks post-game.

### Cited Findings
- Patel et al. 2016, Annals of Internal Medicine: 13-week RCT, 281 overweight/obese employees, 7,000-step daily goal; arms = gain ($1.40/day earned), lottery, loss (money pre-allocated and removed if goal missed), control. Loss arm achieved goal on a 50% relatively higher proportion of days than control; the gain incentive was no better than control; the loss group's step difference vs control was not significant (adjusted +861 steps, P = 0.056); during follow-up steps decreased in all incentive groups and did not differ from control. — [PubMed 26881417](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26881417/); [PMC6029433](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6029433); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20160217/financial-loss-incentive-most-effective-at-increasing-physical-activity); [Penn CHIBE](https://chibe.upenn.edu/news/loss-incentive-motivates-employees-to-take-more-steps)
- STEP UP trial (Patel et al. 2019, JAMA Internal Medicine): 602 overweight/obese adults across the US, 36 weeks (24-week game + 12-week follow-up with no game). Game used behavioural-economics design with points and levels for step goals. All three gamified arms (support, collaboration, competition) beat control during the intervention: +689, +637, +920 steps/day respectively (competition 95% CI 513–1,328). In follow-up only competition stayed above control (+569 steps, 95% CI 142–996). Funded jointly by Deloitte and the Penn Medicine Nudge Unit. — [PMC6735420](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20190909/gamification-may-improve-effectiveness-of-weight-loss-interventions); [ACC quick take](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2019/11/26/08/23/key-takeaways-regarding-gamification-to-increase-physical-activity); [Penn Medicine release](https://www.pennmedicine.org/news/news-releases/2019/september/using-a-wearable-device-to-exercise-more-add-competition-to-improve-your-results)
- Financial-incentive meta-analyses on durability: Mitchell et al. 2020 found modest incentives (~US$1.40/day) increased PA over short and long interventions and after removal (pooled +513.8 steps post-intervention), contrary to earlier reports; a separate 2021 AJHP meta-analysis of 51 trials found only a "slight" sustained increase in leisure-time PA after cessation, with low certainty of evidence; a 2025 update notes evidence on long-term follow-up effects remains limited. — [Mitchell et al. 2020 BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/54/21/1259.full.pdf); [IWH summary](https://iwh.on.ca/node/3123); [PubMed 32672050](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32672050/)

### Inferences
- Loss framing works in the short term with real money. Whether in-game loss (e.g. world decay, "cooling") recruits the same loss aversion is untested here; the closest analogue (STEP UP's points that could be lost/levels that could drop) was bundled with social elements, so the loss mechanic's isolated effect is unknown.
- For a non-competitive single-player game, the only arm that stayed durable in STEP UP (competition) is the one the design excludes; durable effects should not be assumed.
- Loss/decay mechanics risk introjected (guilt/pressure) motivation, which Teixeira et al. found less consistently related to exercise than autonomous motivation; mild, recoverable decay is lower risk than punitive, irreversible loss.

### Gaps
- No RCT found isolating virtual (non-monetary) loss/decay mechanics for walking in adults.
- Patel's other gamification trials (e.g. 2017 families trial; 2021 BE-ACTIVE) were not retrieved in this session.

## Streaks, the what-the-hell effect, repair, fresh starts and goal gradients

### Takeaway
Seeing an intact streak increases continued engagement, and seeing a broken one decreases it, independently of actual behaviour; the damage is reduced when the break can be repaired or blamed on an external cause. Rewarding a return after a missed session was the top intervention in a 61,000-person gym megastudy, and temporal landmarks (new week/month) naturally boost exercise starts.

### Cited Findings
- Silverman & Barasch 2023, Journal of Consumer Research (7 studies): highlighting an intact streak in a behaviour log increased subsequent engagement vs highlighting a broken streak, independent of actual past behaviour ("depends solely on how that behavior is represented"); effect amplified when people felt personally responsible for the break and diminished when they could repair it. — [INSEAD publication page](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer); [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive)
- Exercise example from that work: about 66% chose another strength exercise when the streak was intact vs about 58% when broken. — [Psychology Today (Markman)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- Qualitative study of the Lift habit app: reminders and streaks support repetition but create dependency and fragility; users often abandon the app and disengage. — [Northumbria research portal](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/dont-kick-the-habit-the-role-of-dependency-in-habit-formation-app/)
- What-the-hell effect: originated in dieting research (a lapse treated as total failure leading to goal abandonment); tendency to give up vs course-correct appears to be a stable individual difference (Zemack-Rugar); evidence its strength may be smaller than first thought (Tomiyama et al. 2009). — [Spring.org.uk summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=13881); [EFPSA research blog](https://more.efpsa.org/rpblog/?p=1172)
- Sharif & Shu (OBHDP 2019): framing goals with an "emergency reserve" (a small number of allowed skips) decreased the detrimental impact of subgoal failure on goal attainment. — [Sharif & Shu manuscript (UCLA Anderson)](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf) (manuscript version; check published OBHDP article)
- Milkman et al. 2021 Nature megastudy: 54 four-week programmes, >61,000 24 Hour Fitness members; 45% of interventions increased weekly visits by 9–27%; the top performer gave small rewards (points) for returning to the gym after a missed workout; only 8% of interventions produced change measurable after the four weeks; expert forecasters failed to predict which would work. — [Scimex summary](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/which-programs-give-gym-goers-the-biggest-boost); [Caltech record](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/mys88-cc756); [Nature News & Views PDF](https://media.nature.com/original/magazine-assets/d41586-021-03400-x/d41586-021-03400-x.pdf); [HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/megastudies-improve-impact-applied-behavioural-science)
- Fresh-start effect (Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014, Management Science): gym attendance at Penn rose at the start of a new week, month, semester and year and after birthdays (except 21st); mechanism: landmarks open new "mental accounting periods" that relegate past failures to a previous period. — [Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014 (PDF)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:60:y:2014:i:10:p:2563-2582)
- Goal-gradient (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006, JMR): effort accelerates as people approach a reward; customers given a 12-stamp coffee card with 2 bonus stamps pre-filled completed 10 purchases faster than those with a blank 10-stamp card ("illusionary goal progress"), not explained by sunk cost; in a music-rating study participants visited more often and persisted longer near the goal. — [Kivetz et al. 2006 (PDF)](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf); [Columbia summary](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal)

### Inferences
- A weekly (rather than daily) goal naturally gives a built-in fresh start every week and reduces the chance of a "broken" representation from one missed day.
- Streak repair/freezes and allowed skips are supported indirectly (Silverman & Barasch repair moderator; Sharif & Shu reserves); a "welcome back" reward for returning after a gap has the strongest field evidence (Milkman megastudy).
- Showing progress toward the current week's goal (with some head start) leverages the goal gradient; a decay mechanic that visibly "breaks" the world risks the broken-streak demotivation.
- Avoid framing a missed week as failure attributable to the player; framing as neutral/external reduces the drop in engagement.

### Gaps
- No RCT found comparing streak freezes vs no freezes on long-term PA.
- Effect sizes for the fresh-start gym study not retrieved.

## Habit formation timelines and whether rewards help habits stick

### Takeaway
Habits take weeks to months: about 66 days median to peak automaticity for daily health behaviours (range 18 to an extrapolated 254), and about six weeks of at least four sessions per week for exercise; consistency matters more than perfection, and a single missed occasion barely matters.

### Cited Findings
- Lally et al. 2010 (EJSP): 96 participants (82 completed) repeating a chosen daily health behaviour in the same context for 84 days; mean time to 95% of peak automaticity 66 days, range 18 to 254 days (upper end extrapolated); "missing one opportunity to perform the behavior did not materially affect the habit formation process"; repeated omissions had cumulative detrimental effects. — [UCL news](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-long-does-it-really-take-to-form-a-habit/)
- Kaushal & Rhodes 2015 (J Behav Med): 111 new gym members surveyed over 12 weeks; at least four exercise sessions per week for six weeks was the minimum needed to establish an exercise habit; consistency was the strongest predictor; authors recommend simple, enjoyable sessions focused on consistency. — [PubMed 25851609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/); [UVic PDF](https://www.uvic.ca/research/labs/bmed/assets/docs/Kaushal,%20Rhodes,%202015.pdf)
- Durability of gamified/incentivised behaviour beyond the intervention is weak in general: only 8% of 54 megastudy interventions had measurable effects after the 4-week programme. — [Scimex](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/which-programs-give-gym-goers-the-biggest-boost)

### Inferences
- A game intended to build a habit should keep the player engaged for at least 2–3 months; early weeks matter most.
- Missing one day/week should be designed as harmless (Lally), but long gaps should prompt gentle re-entry.
- Stable cues (same time/place) plus consistency should be emphasised over volume; rewards that encourage frequency rather than distance/intensity fit habit evidence better.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence found on whether in-game rewards accelerate automaticity or survive reward removal; Lally's paper itself not retrieved in full (figures from secondary coverage).

## Gamification meta-analyses for physical activity: effect sizes and durability

### Takeaway
Gamification yields a small-to-medium short-term increase in PA (g about 0.42, roughly +1,600 steps/day) that shrinks to a very small effect (g about 0.15) about 14 weeks after interventions end.

### Cited Findings
- Mazeas et al. 2022, JMIR (16 RCTs, 2,407 participants, PROSPERO CRD42020186882): pooled g = 0.42 (95% CI 0.14–0.69); vs inactive controls g = 0.58 (0.08–1.07); vs active non-gamified PA interventions g = 0.23 (0.05–0.41); steps MD +1,609.56/day (372.39–2,846.73); MVPA g = 0.31 (-0.19 to 0.80, not significant); long-term effect at mean ~14 weeks post-intervention g = 0.15 (0.07–0.23); no moderation by age, gender, BMI, or healthy vs chronic disease. — [Mazeas et al. 2022, JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/E26779/) (some figures from accepted-manuscript text; verify against final version)
- Xu et al. 2022, JMIR mHealth and uHealth: 50 studies reviewed, 15 RCTs meta-analysed; authors describe mixed findings for gamification on PA and sedentary behaviour. — [Xu et al. 2022 (PDF)](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2022/2/e27794/PDF); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/a53185d40846438b82e7f34a72e9f139)
- Behaviourally designed gamification in STEP UP (+637 to +920 steps/day during game; only competition durable at 12 weeks post) is consistent with the meta-analytic picture of short-term benefit and modest durability. — [PMC6735420](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420)

### Inferences
- Expect roughly +600 to +1,600 steps/day while the game is engaging, decaying toward baseline once it is not; durability depends on whether players keep playing and whether a habit forms.
- Gamification adds value over non-gamified PA programmes (g 0.23 vs active controls), so mechanics matter beyond simple tracking.

### Gaps
- Xu et al. pooled effect sizes not retrieved (site not reachable from this session).
- Johnson et al. 2016 (Internet Interventions systematic review of gamification for health) and Bassett-Gunter work were not retrieved; no figures reported here.
- Few meta-analyses break effects down by individual game element (points, badges, streaks, narrative).

## Goal difficulty, feedback, implementation intentions and framing

### Takeaway
Goal setting has a medium effect on PA (d about 0.55), and implementation intentions (if-then plans) a small-to-medium one (SMD about 0.31 post, 0.24 at follow-up), larger when barrier planning is included. Evidence on progress-vs-deficit framing for PA was not found.

### Cited Findings
- McEwan et al. 2016, Health Psychology Review: 45 articles, 52 interventions, 126 effect sizes; goal-setting interventions had a medium positive effect on PA, d = 0.552 (95% CI 0.43–0.67), consistent across delivery modes, intervention lengths and populations. — [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-multi-component-goal-setting-interventions-f/); [PDF](https://eclass.uth.gr/modules/document/file.php/PE_P_160/2022%20goal%20setting%2C%20imagery%2C%20attention%20Yannis%20Theodorakis/2020%20The%20effectiveness%20of%20multi%20component%20goal%20setting%20interventions%20for%20changing%20physical%20activity%20behaviour%20a%20systematic%20review%20and%20meta%20analysis.pdf)
- Epton et al. 2017 meta-analysis across behaviours: small unique effect of goal setting (d = 0.34), stronger when goals were difficult, set publicly, or set for groups. — [PubMed 29189034](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189034/)
- Bélanger-Gravel, Godin & Amireault 2013 (26 studies): implementation intentions on PA SMD = 0.31 (0.11–0.51) post-intervention and 0.24 (0.13–0.35) at follow-up; follow-up length did not reduce the effect; larger with barrier management. — [McMaster Optimal Aging summary](https://www.mcmasteroptimalaging.org/full-article/he/meta-analytic-review-effect-implementation-intentions-physical-activity-25322)
- Positive (informational) feedback raised intrinsic motivation in Deci et al.'s meta-analysis (d = 0.33). — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)

### Inferences
- A personalised, moderately challenging weekly goal plus a prompt to plan when/where to walk (and what to do if it rains) is well supported.
- Feedback framed as progress and competence (what grew) aligns with the positive-feedback evidence; deficit-framed warnings ("your world is shrinking") may be effective short-term by loss aversion but risk pressure-based motivation.

### Gaps
- Locke & Latham primary sources and moderator data on proximal vs distal goals in PA were not retrieved; McEwan moderator results on goal proximity/feedback not confirmed.
- No PA-specific RCT found comparing progress-framed vs deficit-framed feedback.
- Adaptive vs fixed goals (e.g. Patel-style baseline-plus-increment goals) not researched in this session.
