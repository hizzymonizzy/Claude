# Let the goal stretch, keep the floor fixed

A non-competitive walking game should treat the health guideline as a fixed floor and let the adaptive goal set only the stretch target above it. Both WHO and US guidelines set the same adult target: **150–300 moderate-intensity minutes a week, with a vigorous minute worth two**. Activity can be spread across the week in any pattern and in bouts of any length. That makes a weekly active-minutes goal the best-supported design unit. Small trials show adaptive goals beating fixed ones by about **1,000 steps a day**, and they do best when paired with small, immediate rewards. Loss-framed mechanics also raise activity while they run, but the gains mostly fade when the mechanic stops. Every mainstream app that had a hard failure state later added a pause or slack. No mainstream app reviewed lets earned progress decay, apart from Habitica, whose punishment mechanics hurt motivation when users judged them unfair. Wander World's cooling is already gentler than any of these: losses are small and slow, there is a floor, recovery is symmetric, and rest mode pauses it. Its one structural flaw is that cooling is scored against the adaptive goal. A player whose goal has risen to 300 can therefore cool in a week of 200 minutes, which beats the guideline. The main recommendation is to split the week into three zones. Below 150 the world cools. Between 150 and the adaptive goal it holds. At or above the goal it warms. The named bands (Stroller 150, Rambler 200, Trekker 250, Pathfinder 300) then describe the stretch goal and are kept as achievements, never taken away.

## Guidelines give a 150-minute floor and a 300-minute ceiling, counted weekly

WHO's 2020 guidelines say adults should do "at least **150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity** aerobic physical activity; or at least 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity" activity, "or an equivalent combination", across the week. The guideline adds that "some physical activity is better than none" and that replacing sitting with activity of any intensity, light included, has benefits ([Bull et al. 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/24/1451)). The US Physical Activity Guidelines (2018) set the same range ([Piercy et al. 2018](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2712935)). They also dropped the old 10-minute minimum bout, so **bouts of any duration count** toward the weekly total ([APTA](https://apta.org/news/2018/11/13/new-physical-activity-guidelines-stress-the-importance-of-movement-of-any-duration)). Both guidelines also recommend muscle strengthening on two or more days a week. A walking game cannot meet that part, and it should not claim to.

A weekly window is a sound choice for health and for motivation. For the same total, packing activity into one or two days ("weekend warrior") carried a cardiovascular mortality risk similar to spreading it out: **HR 0.60 vs 0.59** compared with inactive adults ([O'Donovan et al. 2017](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1539729/1/O'Donovan_Association_Weekend_Warrior.pdf)). A US cohort found the same equivalence for all-cause and cause-specific mortality, though the weekend-warrior all-cause estimate was not statistically significant ([dos Santos et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9257680)). Both studies are observational. Industry has converged on the same unit. Fitbit's default is **150 Active Zone Minutes a week**, with cardio and peak minutes counting double ([Google/Fitbit](https://support.google.com/fitbit/answer/14236509)). Google's Heart Points, built with the AHA and WHO, award 1 point per moderate minute and 2 per vigorous minute, against 150 a week ([AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/news/2018/08/21/google-just-launched-heart-points-here-are-5-things-you-need-to-know)). Garmin tracks weekly intensity minutes ([Garmin](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/vivoactive4_4S/EN-US/GUID-018B4A1D-17C8-4F7C-8D10-1521D9D8FC9C.html)).

### Minutes beat steps, but slow walking still earns its place

Steps and minutes measure different things, and the evidence favours a hybrid. Step volume has a strong dose–response link to lower mortality. In a 15-cohort meta-analysis, the top step quartile had **HR 0.47** against the bottom one, levelling off at 6,000–8,000 steps a day for adults over 60 and 8,000–10,000 for younger adults. Walking pace added nothing once total steps were accounted for ([Paluch et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35247352/)). The NHANES and Women's Health Study cohorts also found no independent effect of intensity ([Saint-Maurice et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7093766); [Lee et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31141585/)). Benefit starts low, at about **4,000 steps a day for all-cause and 2,300 for cardiovascular death** ([ESC/Banach et al. 2023](https://escardio.org/The-ESC/Press-Office/Press-releases/World-s-largest-study-shows-the-more-you-walk-the-lower-your-risk-of-death-even-if-you-walk-fewer-than-5-000-steps)). The guideline unit, however, is moderate-to-vigorous minutes. The accepted walking proxy for moderate intensity is about **100 steps per minute**, and about 130 for vigorous ([Tudor-Locke et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-019-0769-6)).

Minutes suit Wander World better than steps. Steps cannot count paddling or cycling. Step counters also miscount impaired gait, so a step-based penalty would punish measurement error ([PMC5902695](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5902695/)). Wander World's active minute counts every recorded walking minute and gives brisk minutes extra weight, with vigorous minutes doubled. This is more generous than the guideline's MVPA minute, because a slow stroll counts in full. The step-volume evidence supports that generosity, since slow walking clearly helps. Honesty matters, though. A week of 150 slow active minutes is not the same as 150 guideline minutes, so the game should call 150 "the guideline's shape", not proof of meeting the guideline. If the watch supplies intensity minutes, the game could also show them for information.

## Adaptive goals outperform fixed ones when they trail behaviour gently

Small randomised trials consistently favour goals that track a person's own recent activity. In one trial, adaptive goals raised steps by 2,728 a day against 1,598 for a static goal, a significant **1,130-step difference** ([Adams et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24349392/)). A machine-learning personalised goal beat a fixed 10,000-step goal by **960 steps a day** over 10 weeks ([Zhou et al. 2018](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/1/e28/)). In the 12-month WalkIT Arizona factorial trial (512 adults), the arm with adaptive goals and immediate rewards gained about **16.5 minutes a day of MVPA** and outperformed both delayed-reward arms ([PMC8820277](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8820277)). The best-documented rule ranks the last 9 days of activity and sets the goal at the **60th percentile** ([WalkIT protocol](https://researchprotocols.org/2015/3/e108/PDF)). The goal therefore sits a little above typical performance, rises as the person improves, and falls on its own after illness. The evidence has limits. An earlier factorial trial was mixed in its first phase ([Adams et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5372290/)). All of these trials used daily step goals, and no meta-analysis isolates adaptive goals. Applying the rule to weekly minutes is an extrapolation.

Goal setting itself has a medium effect on physical activity, **d ≈ 0.55** across 52 interventions ([McEwan et al. 2016](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-multi-component-goal-setting-interventions-f/)). Adding if–then plans helps further (SMD 0.31 after the intervention, 0.24 at follow-up), especially when the plan covers barriers ([Bélanger-Gravel et al. 2013](https://www.mcmasteroptimalaging.org/full-article/he/meta-analytic-review-effect-implementation-intentions-physical-activity-25322)). Goals should rise gradually, but no specific percentage has evidence behind it. ACSM recommends "gradual progression … for best adherence and least injury risk" ([Garber et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)). In a trial of novice runners, a 10%-per-week plan showed no injury benefit (**20.8% vs 20.3%**) ([running-physio summary of Buist et al. 2008](https://www.running-physio.com/skimpyresearch/)). A cohort study hinted at more risk above **30% growth over two weeks** ([Nielsen et al. 2014](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/excessive-progression-in-weekly-running-distance-and-risk-of-runn)). Walking loads are far lower than running loads, so the real hazard is a goal that demands sudden catch-up spikes, not a goal that rises by 10%.

Wander World's current rule rises 10% after three of four weeks at 120% or more of the goal. This is conservative in the right way. The goal trails what the player already does, settling near four-fifths of their typical week, so it never demands load the player has not already shown. The WalkIT 60th-percentile rule sets a harder goal that is met only about four weeks in ten. That stretch is fine when the goal drives rewards. It is not fine when missing the goal causes cooling, which is the crux of the recommendations below.

## Loss works while it runs, then fades, and decay is a rare and risky choice

Loss aversion moves behaviour. In a 13-week trial with a 7,000-step goal, money that was given up front and taken away after a miss raised goal attainment by 50% relative to control. The same amount offered as a gain did no better than control. After the incentive ended, steps in all incentive groups fell back to control level ([Patel et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26881417/)). The STEP UP trial is the closest analogue to weekly cooling. Players started each week with 70 points, lost 10 for each missed day, and moved up or down levels weekly. All gamified arms beat control by **637–920 steps a day**, but only the competitive arm stayed ahead after the game stopped ([PubMed 31498375](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31498375/)). Wander World leaves out competition by design. Across 16 RCTs, gamification gave **g = 0.42 (about +1,600 steps a day)**, shrinking to **g = 0.15** around 14 weeks after interventions ended ([Mazeas et al. 2022](https://jmir.org/2022/1/e26779)). Pokémon GO's step gain of 955 a day was gone by week 6 ([Karolinska summary of Howe et al. 2016](https://news.ki.se/pokemon-go-only-slightly-improves-physical-activity-among-adults)). The lesson is that a mechanic works only while the game keeps being played. A loop that the player is happy to keep running for years matters more than an intense one.

No controlled study isolates a virtual decay mechanic, so the safety case rests on nearby evidence. In a study of the habit RPG Habitica, **all 45 users experienced counterproductive effects**. A common one was being punished during productive periods for things they could not check off. How unfair users judged the reward system predicted how much their motivation fell ([Diefenbach & Müssig 2019](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/77668)). When Fitbit removed challenges in 2023, earned trophies vanished with them, and users protested across 49+ forum pages ([Fitbit Community](https://community.fitbit.com/t5/Product-Feedback/Don-t-remove-Challenges-amp-Adventures/idi-p/5335785/page/49)). That loss had nothing to do with performance, yet users still felt it as punishment. The games take the opposite approach. Pokémon GO's weekly tiers simply forfeit a bonus when missed ([Niantic](https://niantic.helpshift.com/hc/faq/3265-adventure-sync)), and Pikmin Bloom gives a participation prize even when a challenge fails ([Pikmin wiki](https://www.pikminwiki.com/Weekly_challenge)). Measurement itself carries a cost. Pedometer users walked more but enjoyed walking less, unless the activity was framed by its intrinsic value ([Etkin 2016](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps)). Tracker "dependence" is strongest in people with extrinsic motivation, a high need for closure and low hope of success ([Attig & Franke 2019](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/i-track-therefore-i-walk-exploring-the-motivational-costs-of-wear/)). A 2026 study of adults with OCD found that streaks and progress bars fed "not-just-right" compulsions ([JMIR 2026](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e85033/PDF)).

Decay is still defensible here, for reasons the evidence supports. Wander World's world growth is symbolic, informational feedback. In the meta-analysis of 128 experiments, that kind of reward raised intrinsic motivation (positive feedback d = 0.33). Expected, performance-contingent tangible rewards were the kind that undermined it ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)). The player agreed to the cooling rule. That passes the persuasive-technology "golden rule", which says never to persuade users toward something they would not consent to ([Berdichevsky & Neuenschwander 1999](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/1999/5/7904-toward-an-ethics-of-persuasive-technology/fulltext)). It keeps passing only while the rule stays visible, fair and under the player's control. UK regulators name **confirmshaming and biased framing** as harmful design ([ICO/CMA 2023](https://www.drcf.org.uk/siteassets/drcf/pdf-files/harmful-design-in-digital-markets-ico-cma-joint-position-paper.pdf)). That makes a useful checklist for copy such as "your world is dying".

## Streaks break, habits bend, and every vendor ended up adding slack

How a lapse is shown matters as much as the lapse itself. Showing people an intact streak raised further engagement and showing a broken one lowered it, regardless of what they actually did. The drop was smaller when the break could be repaired ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer)). Framing a goal with an "emergency reserve" of allowed skips reduced the damage from failing a subgoal ([Sharif & Shu 2019](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf)). In a megastudy of 61,000 gym members, the best of 54 programmes gave small rewards for **returning after a missed workout**. Only 8% of programmes had effects that lasted past four weeks ([Scimex summary of Milkman et al. 2021](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/which-programs-give-gym-goers-the-biggest-boost)). New weeks and months act as "fresh starts" that put past failures in a closed account ([Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)). Effort also speeds up as a goal comes into view, and a head start strengthens that effect ([Kivetz et al. 2006](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf)).

Habits form slowly and tolerate misses. Reaching 95% of peak automaticity took a median of **66 days** (range 18–254). "Missing one opportunity … did not materially affect" the process, though repeated misses added up ([UCL summary of Lally et al. 2010](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)). For exercise, new gym members needed **at least four sessions a week for six weeks**, and consistency was the strongest predictor ([Kaushal & Rhodes 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)). So frequency should earn more reward than heroic single outings. One bad week should cost almost nothing, while a long run of bad weeks should lead to gentle re-entry, not to accumulated penalties.

The industry learned this through complaints. Apple added the option to pause Activity rings for a day, week, month or longer "without affecting award streaks", along with per-weekday goals, in watchOS 11 ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/121163)). The trade press noted this came "after 9 years" of user requests ([iMore](https://www.imore.com/health-fitness/apple-watch/after-9-years-apple-has-finally-added-the-apple-watch-feature-weve-been-begging-for-rest-days-are-here-and-they-wont-break-your-award-streaks)). Duolingo added streak freezes because broken streaks demotivate, and its own A/B tests showed retention gains ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). Habitica added "Rest in the Inn" to pause damage ([Habitica Wiki](https://habitica.fandom.com/wiki/Rest_in_the_Inn)). Fitbit's Daily Readiness turns a low-readiness day into a recovery suggestion rather than a failure ([Google/Fitbit](https://support.google.com/fitbit/answer/14236710)). Illness is the hard case. Sports-medicine sources advise against exercising with fever or below-the-neck symptoms and recommend a staged return that starts with walking. The popular "neck check" rule itself has little evidence behind it ([University of Pretoria SEMLI](https://www.up.ac.za/Sport-Exercise-Medicine-and-Lifestyle-Institute/news/post_3042925-medical-guidance-for-athletes-returning-to-sport-after-an-acute-respiratory-illness)). After a lapse, self-compassion predicts re-engagement with exercise goals and lower negative affect ([Semenchuk et al. 2018](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Semenchuk2018.pdf)). Perfectionists are the least likely to respond to themselves that way ([Biskas et al. 2022](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/184697/1/British%20J%20Social%20Psychol%20-%202022%20-%20Biskas%20-%20Using%20social%20cognition%20models%20to%20understand%20why%20people%20%20such%20as%20perfectionists%20.pdf)). Perfectionists are also the users a closure-seeking penalty would hurt most.

## Recommendations for Wander World's weekly goal and cooling

The evidence points to one structural change and several smaller adjustments. The structural change gives the player's request ("adapt, but only for health goals") a precise meaning. **The health floor decides cooling. The adaptive goal decides warming.** Under the current rule, a goal of 300 means a 200-minute week falls below 80% and cools ⅛ step. That week exceeds the guideline, and punishing it is exactly the Habitica pattern of being penalised while doing well. Splitting the week into zones removes the problem and keeps the adaptive goal motivating.

| Week's active minutes | Zone | World effect | Copy tone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Below 75 (half the floor) | Cooling | ¼ step cools (unchanged size) | "A quiet week; the roads cooled a little" |
| 75–119 | Cooling, light | ⅛ step | same, smaller |
| 120–149 | Near miss | ~1/40 step | "Almost there" |
| 150 to below goal | Holding | No change; counts toward band progress | "Kept warm" |
| At or above goal | Warming | ¼ step restored (current rule); faded places still count double | "The world warmed" |

The bands should name the stretch goal. They should not form a ladder of jumps. Each band starts where a guideline marker sits: **Stroller 150** at the guideline minimum, **Pathfinder 300** at the top of the range where extra benefit is documented, and Rambler 200 and Trekker 250 between them. Moving straight from 150 to 200 would be a 33% jump. So the goal should keep moving in steps of about 10%, rounded to 10 minutes, and the band name should change when the goal crosses its threshold. Crossing a threshold is a competence moment worth celebrating. The highest band reached should be kept as a title that never goes away, just as gold is never reduced. The Fitbit trophy backlash shows that taking away earned symbols reads as punishment. The current band still shows the live goal. The rules should never set the goal above 300. A player who walks more is welcome to, but nothing pushes them further.

The adaptation rule can stay close to the current one, with four changes. First, compute rises and eases only from scored weeks. Rest-mode weeks, the grace period, and weeks with sensor or sync gaps the player did not cause should be excluded from both windows. Second, keep rises slow: at most one 10% rise per four weeks, and never above the median of the last four scored weeks plus 10%, so the goal follows proven load. Third, keep eases fast: two scored weeks under 70% bring the goal down to their average, never below 150. Because cooling is now tied to the floor, a lower goal makes warming easier again without lowering the health bar. Fourth, announce every change on Tuesday with the rule spelled out ("three of your last four weeks were well above 200, so your goal is now 220"). No vendor publishes its adaptive rule clearly, and Habitica showed that a rule felt to be unfair erodes motivation. The lock stays, and the player should also be able to pick a band directly. Both serve the autonomy that self-determination research links to lasting exercise motivation ([Teixeira et al. 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3441783/)). If the player wants more stretch, the WalkIT 60th-percentile rule over the last six to eight scored weeks is the evidence-backed alternative. It is only safe because the zone split means a missed stretch goal never cools anything.

Cooling should keep its current safeguards and gain a few more. The safeguards to keep are the small per-week losses, the four-week lag before a visible step is lost, the floor two steps below best, symmetric recovery, double credit for faded places, the 14-day grace and the rest pause. Rest mode should work like Apple's: one tap, no reason asked, with presets for this week, until a chosen date, and open-ended. Leaving rest mode should start a **return ramp**. For three scored weeks the cooling floor drops to 75 and any week at or above 75 counts as holding. This matches the staged return after illness and the guidance that some activity is better than none. The first walk after a gap of seven days or more should earn a small, visible "welcome back" bonus, the best-performing tactic in the gym megastudy. Its effect is the fresh-start, return-after-a-miss pattern rather than a recovery the player has to earn. Tuesday copy should be neutral and look forward. It should show what was kept (best level, floor, places that never fade) next to what cooled, use no red alarm styling, and send no loss-framed notifications. Cooling should look seasonal, like frost on a road, not like ruin. A setting to turn cooling off entirely protects the users most at risk from closure mechanics, and it costs a single-player game nothing. Copy should never suggest "making up" minutes in a weekend push. Weekend-warrior patterns are healthy, but a catch-up spike is the pattern linked to injury.

## Conclusion

Decay itself is not the main risk in Wander World's design, because the cooling is already gentler than anything the evidence warns about. The risk is coupling. Once an adaptive goal also sets the penalty threshold, success raises the bar for punishment, and a healthy 200-minute week can be scored as failure. Separating the fixed guideline floor from the adaptive stretch goal resolves this. Loss aversion then guards the minimum that matters for health, and everything above the minimum is reward. That is also the clearest reading of "adapt, but only for health goals".

The evidence has clear limits. No trial tests virtual decay, weekly adaptive goals, or bands named for guideline levels, and gamification effects fade with novelty. The design should therefore be judged on whether it stays pleasant enough to keep playing for months, the timescale on which habits form. The game should record its own effect on the player's weekly minutes over the first 8–12 weeks, using the diagnostics it already has. Those figures will say more about this player than any of the trials above.
